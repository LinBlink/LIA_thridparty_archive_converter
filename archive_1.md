On December 9, 1986, 24-year-old Linda Cook, a resident of Portsmouth, England, was brutally attacked close to an area of wasteland known as “Merry Row”. Raped and strangled, her body was found later that same day. The subsequent police investigation focused on Michael Shirley, an 18-year-old able seaman who spent the next 16 year in prison for a
crime
it was proven he did not commit.
Just after midnight on the evening of December 9, 1986, Linda Cook was walking home through Buckland after visiting a friend. Located in the middle of Portsmouth, and only a mile from the sea, Buckland is an area of patchwork estates. The 24-year-old barmaid was living at the home of Linda Gray on Victoria Road North, Portsmouth, but she would never make it.
At some point before she arrived, sometime between 00:30am and 01:00am, she was attacked on an area of wasteland known as “Merry Row”, adjacent to Lake Road. During a vicious attack that lasted approximately fifteen minutes, Cook was subjected to prolonged physical attack causing her death.
Linda Cook was found later that same morning, on a patch of wasteland known as Merry Row, the brutality of the assault evident on her naked body. Cook had been strangled to death. Her killer had stamped down several times with such force that her jaw and spine were fractured and her larynx crushed. On her abdomen were left the imprints of the killers right athletic shoe.
With Cook’s underwear found nearby, it was suspected the crime had been committed by a sexual predator. After a pathologist took a number of vaginal, anal, and vulval swabs for forensic examination, which soon confirmed the presence of semen, while Cook’s underwear tested negative.
A number of other evidence was gathered from the body, including fibres beneath Cook’s fingernails. This was despite the fact that the pathologist noted that Cook’s fingernails
“were long and unbroken, suggesting that she did not scratch her attacker, or at least did not do so with any ferocity.”
It was the determination of police and forensic experts that
“the semen found inside [her] vagina had been deposited there after her underwear had been removed and consequently was deposited by her attacker.”
All other possibilities that Cook had sexual intercourse before her death were ruled out after her whereabouts since 10:00 the previous morning were confirmed.
After the discovery of Linda Cook’s body, police reacted with speed. The murder came after a series of sex attacks in the area of Buckland, and with mounting public pressure to capture the culprit, who had been nicknamed the “Beast of Buckland” by the press, meant the investigation was given top priority.
Although police had shoe imprint evidence that could help identify the killer, they had no potential suspects and so decided to look for any witnesses who might have seen any suspicious the night of the murder. Officers conducted house-to-house inquiries in the Buckland area, and soon came across a promising lead.
Deena Fogg, a local woman, told police about an encounter she had with a young man the night Linda Cook was murdered. On the night of December 9, 1986, Fogg had been to a nightclub called “Joanna’s”, located in Southsea, Portsmouth, where she had met a young able seaman in the Royal Navy.
Joanna’s was a seafront club popular with sailors on shore leave who would try their luck with local girls. That man, 18-year-old Michael Shirley, was at the time serving aboard HMS Apollo, which was docked in Portsmouth at the time of the murder. Fogg told police that when the club closed she agreed to go to her home with Shirley, and the two shared a taxi.
After a short journey of around 5 minutes, the two arrived at a residential tower block, and Fogg said she told Shirley that she needed to go to her mother’s to collect her young child, and claimed she told Shirley she would come back to the taxi. However, Fogg said she had a change of heart, and had no intention of taking the young seamen back to her flat. Instead she left the building by using another exit and returned to her nearby home alone.
Detectives began working on the theory that Shirley, sexually frustrated by his rejection at the hands of Fogg, roamed the Buckland estate until he came across Linda Cook. With this working hypothesis, Michael Shirley became the prime suspect in the crime. On January 5, 1987, Michael Shirley paid another visit to “Joanna’s” before his ship left the UK.
There he saw Deena Fogg again, who pointed him out to a police detective as the man she encountered the night of Linda Cook’s murder. He was promptly arrested and taken into custody on suspicion of murder. Under questioning, Shirley refused to admit his involvement, and gave police a timeline of his whereabouts that fateful night.
During his police interrogation, Michael Shirley told police that after Deena Fogg had failed to return after fifteen minutes, he realised he had been tricked. He paid the taxi fare and decided to start walking on foot to see if he could find Fogg. He said he spent approximately ten minutes looking for Fogg, before giving up and returning to the ship.
He said that he caught another taxi on Edinburgh Road at precisely 01:23am, which took him to the gates of the dock where he was booked-in back aboard ship at 01:45am. Two days later Shirley said that by chance he met Fogg again, and the two had a brief conversation, which Fogg told police she found “intimidating,” because they spoke about the murder of Linda Cook, and were in close proximity to the crime scene.
Shirley subsequently spent his Christmas shore leave at his parents’ home in Leamington Spa, Warwickshire, and in early January 1987 he returned to Portsmouth harbour and was due to sail to the Falkland Islands. It then, shortly before the ship was due to leave, that he paid another visit to “Joanna’s” and was placed under arrest.
During a subsequent search of his parents home, officers found a pair of size 44 athletic shoes with the same tread as those worn by the killer and matched the impressions found on the victims body. That particular shoe was a size 43-45, and had a distinctive tread pattern including the word “Flash” in the heel area.
When asked about the shoes, Shirley stated that he “may” have been wearing them on the night of the murder. When police researched the type of shoe worn by the killer, they found that for a period of three years between 1983 and 1986, 185,000 pairs had been imported into the UK.
In the year 1986, it was found that upwards of 9,000 pairs had been sold in the UK, of which 250 had been purchased in Portsmouth. According to Shirley, he bought the shoes around October 1986. After thorough examination by forensic scientists, no traces of blood from the victim were found on Shirley’s shoes. Because of the importance of the “Flash shoe” evidence, the case was referred to by the press as the “Cinderella murder”.
Neil Humber, a former journalist, said of Shirley’s arrest.
“Once his name was in the frame and he was arrested, the police became blinkered, fitting the crime around him rather than seeing if he did in fact fit the crime.”
Police began to centre their case solely around Michael Shirley, and the evidence soon mounted against him.
The semen samples taken from Linda Cook’s body proved a match with Shirley’s O positive blood group, which is shared by 23.3% of the British adult male population. This was considered by police as another piece of damning evidence, despite the fact that no blood matching Cook’s was found on his clothing, nor were there any matching fibres transferred from Cook’s clothing to Shirley’s, and vice versa.
Charged with the murder of Linda Cook, the trial of Michael Shirley began on January 18, 1988 at Winchester Crown Court. With Mr Justice Hutchison presiding, Shirley maintained his innocence throughout the proceedings. The case for the prosecution, based largely on circumstantial evidence, hindered on the testimony of Deena Fogg.
The four main pieces of circumstantial evidence against Shirley were the athletic shoes found at his parents home, which matched the ones worn by the killer, as well as the killer and Shirley sharing the same blood type. Beyond these two coincidences, the prosecution contended that Shirley attacked Linda Cook, and had in turn been injured by the victim attempting to defend herself.
Indeed, when Shirley was arrested, it was found that he had healed scratches on his face. These included marks on his
“right cheek, right eyebrow, collar bone, left shoulder, right elbow, right forearm, right index finger, left upper arm, and left elbow,”
all of which the prosecution’s expert claimed were roughly four weeks old.
This put the scratch marks having been made with the time frame of the murder. According to a consultant forensic physician who testified for the defence, some of the scratches had been received while Shirley was serving in Barbados in October 1986. Similarly, the defence argued the marks to Michael Shirley’s face could not have been caused by the victim.
When Linda Cook’s body was examined, it was found that her long fingernails were unbroken, suggesting that she had not scratched her attacker with any force. There was no trace evidence found beneath Cook’s nails, in the form of fibres, that linked her death to Shirley. Despite this revelation, the jury were not informed of this discovery.
The last piece of evidence the prosecution put forward to prove Shirley’s guilt centered on the a thirty minute discrepancy in the timeline that became known as the “Missing half-hour”. This arose because of the difference between Fogg’s testimony and that of Shirley’s version of events.
According to Fogg, the two had caught a taxi from “Joanna’s” at just after midnight, and she had arrived home at about 00:15am. When Shirley described his whereabouts that morning, he said his journey back to HMS Apollo saw arrive at 1:45am. However, the prosecution maintained that he should have arrived back at 1:15am, giving rise to the missing 30 minutes that could not be accounted for.
This, the prosecution argued, was sufficient time to have carried out the murder of Linda Cook. However, it would later emerge that Fogg had given two different statements to the police. In the first she had said she left the nightclub with Shirley at around 00:30am, which would have placed Shirley far enough away from the scene when Cook was killed, corroborating his account of the evening and proving his innocence.
Once again, the jury were not informed about the first statement, and it only emerged later that the second statement had been
“made under some duress; she had been in the police station for 10 hours, was concerned for her baby and her mother was standing outside calling for her.”
Another piece of evidence not presented at the trial was the log book of the taxi driver who picked up Fogg and Shirley, which stated he had made a pre-booked pick up at 00:15am and did not arrive at the taxi rank where he collected them until 00.25am. Undoubtedly, the prosecution’s star witness was Deena Fogg.
Her testimony brought into question Shirley’s alibi after she told the court that during their brief discussion when the pair a second time at the nightclub, she got the impression that Shirley was attempting to reinforce his later testimony that they had left the nightclub very late.
The prosecution described this as
“the beginning of an attempt to concoct an alibi.”
It was put to the jury that when giving his statements to the police, Shirley referred to Deena Fogg as “Sue”, which, it later emerged, she had told him was her name. The prosecution contended
“that he [Shirley] was deliberately concealing his knowledge of the girl’s identity in the hope that he would prevent the police from tracing her”.
In contrast to this assertion, Shirley had given
“a full account to the police about his activities”
with Deena Fogg, withholding no information in his statement. He told police that the two had met, and travelled to her home with the intention of having sexual intercourse, but Fogg failed to return after making an excuse.
In the closing statement, the prosecution submitted that it was at this time that after failing to locate Ms Fogg, Michael Shirley
“saw Miss Cook who was walking along Merry Row… and in his frustrated and angry state he attacked her, raped her and murdered her before going back to HMS Apollo.”
For his part, Shirley maintained his innocence throughout the trial. With the largely circumstantial evidence, coupled with the unreliable evidence given by Fogg and the fact that the prosecution produced no forensic evidence against Shirley, his legal team believed that a conviction would not be forthcoming.
Unbeknown to the defence, the jury’s deliberations sided with the prosecution’s case, and after retiring at 10:08am
“with strong hints that the judge wanted a verdict that day,”
they returned at 16:42pm with an 11–1 verdict of guilty. On January 28, 1988, Michael Shirley was found guilty of the rape and murder of Linda Cook and sentenced to life imprisonment.
The defence put forward an application for leave to appeal against the conviction, however this was rejected on May 4, 1989. The judge who turned down the application, Sir David Croom-Johnson, maintained that his decision was influenced by the fact there were
“no lingering doubts”
over the question of Shirley’s guilt.
During his time in prison, Michael Shirley continued to maintain his innocence, and at one point in 1992, he staged a protest on the roof of HMP Long Lartin in order to draw attention to the case, which then escalated to a full-blown hunger strike that lasted five weeks and nearly cost him his life.
In September 1992, Shirley’s solicitors sent fresh evidence to the Home Office, which they claimed pointed to his innocence. In frustration that this evidence, uncovered by a journalist, was largely being ignored, Shirley staged his protest. He later said,
“Those couple of days on the roof were my only nights out in 16 years.”
He only agreed to come down once prison authorities agreed to let in the journalist, Neil Humber, to talk with him. Humber was one of the earliest campaigners convinced of Shirley’s innocence, and his long campaign had formed the basis of Shirley’s appeal. He was the first to call attention to the discrepancy between Fogg’s two police statements, calling this concealed evidence “horrendous”.
“My subsequent investigation and the uncovering of a taxi log proved the police had quite simply ‘shifted time’ to fit their narrative,”
said Humber. Shirley began another hunger strike on January 28, 1993, and his mother told the press that he would remain on hunger strike
“until he gets something in black and white to show that there really is something happening.”
“Hampshire are not going to own up to making a mistake. I would have preferred another force,”
she added. The hunger strike lasted for 42 days, and only ended when the Home Office
“agreed ‘possibly’ to review the case if they were given new evidence in a coherent form.”
Humber argued that a DNA sample taken from the crime scene, that was to undergo fresh tests, was not forthcoming from the police.
“I did pester them on three occasions, asking if any forensic samples could be used to test for DNA,”
he says.
“Twice I was promised they’d look into it, and eventually I was told they’d all been destroyed. This was subsequently found to be untrue.”
Humber was told by his employers that he would be dismissed if he took time off to write the report on the new case, instead of attending a course. He subsequently prepared a 49-page report on the DNA evidence, which was passed to the authorities, and as a result he was fired by his newspaper.
Presented with the evidence, Hampshire Constabulary said
“These matters have been passed to the Chief Constable of Hampshire and a further report will be submitted to the Home Office when these aspects have been inquired into.”
Shirley’s solicitor, Pal Sanghera, told how he was
“disturbed that the same force that had carried out the initial investigation and a further inquiry last summer was being given the task.”
Despite the report compiled by Humber, Michael Shirley’s campaign suffered numerous setbacks in attempting to persuade the government to re-examine fresh evidence. In 1995, he again applied to the Home Office for a review of his conviction but was informed by the Home Secretary Michael Howard that he
“was not minded to refer his case to the Court of Appeal.”
For some, Michael Shirley’s conviction was a staggering miscarriage of Justice. In 1992, his case was highlighted as one of 110 possible miscarriages of justice in a report presented to the Home Office by the National Association of Probation Officers and justice groups Liberty and Conviction.
During his initial imprisonment, Shirley was held in the Category C prison at HM Prison Aylesbury, before being held at the Category A prisons at HM Prison Long Lartin and HM Prison Whitemoor, the latter of which he was imprisoned until 1997, when he was moved to the Category B HM Prison Gartree.
The Category A prisons were reserved only for inmates
“whose escape would be highly dangerous to the public, or the police or the security of the State, and for whom the aim must be to make escape impossible”
. His terms at these prisons were deemed necessary because authorities considered him to be
“in denial”
over his guilt.
Shirley’s lawyer, Anita Bromley, said of her client, “He is not in denial, he denies committing the crime. What Michael’s case shows is how easy it is to be convicted of murder, and how very difficult it is to overturn that conviction.” For authorities, Shirley’s refusal to admit to the crime ensured his prolonged imprisonment.
After serving the minimum tariff of 15 years, Shirley would have been released from prison had he confessed committing the killing to the parole board, but always denied culpability, saying,
“I would have died in prison rather than admit something I didn’t do. I was prepared to stay in forever if necessary to prove my innocence.”
The campaign for Shirley’s release was hampered by the poor handling of evidence by Hampshire Constabulary after the trial. With advances in DNA profiling, the force could have utilised such methods in the investigation and demonstrated Shirley’s innocence. During his time in prison, Michael Shirley
“consistently asked that DNA tests be carried out”.
However, the police claimed that none of the evidence or swabs had been retained, and that much of the remaining evidence had been destroyed some six months after the trial. In 2001, shortly before his transfer to HM Prison Wormwood Scrubs, police publicly admitted that they had located a slide taken from one of the swabs.
This piece of evidence, which police claimed had been misplaced
“in a drawer,”
was tested along with further clothing evidence found in 2002. James Plaskitt, the then MP for Warwick and Leamington said in 2002 that,
“This long delay has imposed an intolerable strain on Michael and his family…”
“The system failed in the original trial, and it has been decidedly sluggish in helping him clear his name,”
Plaskitt said. New tests were carried out of the newly discovered evidence, revealing that Shirley’s DNA profile coincided with only one thin band – and that characteristic is shared by almost a quarter of British men.
The rest of the DNA did not match either Michael Shirley or Linda Cook. His solicitor argued that because the appeal judges had accepted that there were not two attackers, Shirley could not have been the killer. Michael Shirley, who had entered prison at the age of 18, was released in his thirty-fourth year.
Shortly after Shirley’s conviction was quashed, the case was reopened, and Hampshire police appealed for new information. Retired policeman, Chris Clark, argued that because of the initial failings, Hampshire police have done little to shed light on the case since.
“They have never really bothered to progress this case,”
he says.
“This runs true to form with several miscarriages of justice which occurred around the country as early back as 1972-3.”
Clark began independently researching Linda Cook’s murder for a new book, and came across a potential suspect. Clark stated his belief that convicted murderer and rapist, Paul Barry Taylor was responsible for Cook’s murder.
In 1979, Taylor murdered 22-year-old Sally Ann McGrath after meeting her in a hotel bar. After launching a brutal attack, Taylor dumped her body in a woodland. Like Cook, McGrath was murdered after a spate of sex attacks in the area where she was killed.
“Linda’s murder is very similar to Sally Ann’s in Peterborough,”
says Clark.
“She was stamped on, really nasty injuries, well over the top,”
he added. Paul Taylor managed to escape justice for over three decades before his crimes finally caught up with him in 2012, when he was arrested and found guilty of McGrath’s murder, for which he was sentenced to 18 years.
Along with the murder conviction, Taylor was found guilty of three separate rapes, an attempted rape and a serious sexual assault committed in the lead up to the murder. According to Clark, two weeks after McGrath’s murder, in September of 1979, Taylor attacked his 16-year-old sister-in-law, hitting her on the head with a tin of paint.
“They couldn’t progress the attempted rape, but they did him for actual bodily harm and he went away to prison for two years,”
said Clark. After serving his sentence, Taylor moved south, down to Fleet in Hampshire, before settling in Fareham in 1985, a town just six miles away from Portsmouth.
During his research, Clark found a pattern of sex assaults that followed Taylor down the country, culminating in the 1986 murder of Linda Cook. Because he managed to remain under suspicion for so long, even the police force responsible for bringing him to justice have admitted Taylor may have more victims.
“I think there’s every chance that there are women that we don’t know about who were victims of Taylor. I suspect, probably, rape. Whether there are any murders that Taylor was responsible for would be pure speculation,”
Det Supt Jeff Hill told the BBC during an interview in 2012.
For Chris Clark, the evidence he uncovered concerning Taylor is more than just speculation. After seeing a newspaper story about Taylor as a possible suspect, a witness contacted Clark with potential information. The witness told Clark that on the night of the murder, he was driving home past Merry Row.
He claimed to have witnessed a man matching Taylor’s description who ran out in front of his car, and when the driver stopped abruptly and the man stared through the windscreen before running off. The witness claimed to have made a statement to the police about what he saw at the time, but it was never followed up.
Despite these new leads, Clark has been left frustrated by police inaction. Although he has urged investigators to look into the possibility that Taylor is responsible for Linda Cook’s death, he believes that surviving DNA samples might only be partial and therefore insufficient to link Taylor to the crime.
Having submitted numerous freedom of information requests, all of which have been turned down, Clark says there is no way of finding out what, if anything, the police have done to investigate his evidence.
“I think Hampshire Police are trying to keep the lid firmly closed,”
he says.
“They don’t want to reinvestigate because it’s going to show they missed very good potential suspects in their wild chase to get a result.”
In relation to the new evidence and witness statement presented by Clark, Hampshire police issued a statement, saying:
“A review of all information and evidence available in the Linda Cook murder case is ongoing as part of regular routine work carried out by Hampshire Constabulary.”
They declined to comment on the nature of evidence in relation to the case. Although the killer of Linda Cook remains at large and, officially, unidentified, the miscarriage of justice against Michael Shirley was successfully exposed, allowing an innocent man to rebuilt his life after decades behind bars for a crime it was proven he did not commit.
